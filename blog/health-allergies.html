Allergies Explained: Why Your Body Overreacts, and What Helps

By Dr. Helen Asante · September 18, 2026

Pollen, pet dander, peanuts, dust. For millions of people, harmless substances trigger a cascade of sneezing, itching, or worse. Here is how allergies work and how to manage them.

An allergy is the immune system's overreaction to a substance that is normally harmless. The body mistakes something ordinary, such as pollen, dust, or a particular food, for a threat and mounts a defense against it. That misfired defense is what produces allergy symptoms.

When a person with allergies encounters their trigger, or allergen, the immune system releases chemicals including histamine. Histamine causes the familiar effects: sneezing, a runny or blocked nose, itchy or watering eyes, rashes, and swelling. The reaction can be mildly annoying or, in rare cases, life-threatening.

Common allergens fall into a few groups. Airborne triggers like pollen, dust mites, mold, and pet dander often cause hay fever and asthma symptoms. Food allergens such as peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, and milk are among the most frequent, while insect stings, medicines, and latex affect others.

The most severe reaction is anaphylaxis, a rapid, whole-body response that can cause difficulty breathing, a drop in blood pressure, and collapse. It is a medical emergency. People at risk are often prescribed an adrenaline auto-injector and advised to seek emergency care immediately if symptoms appear.

Management starts with identifying and avoiding triggers, sometimes with the help of allergy testing. Antihistamines, nasal sprays, and other medications relieve everyday symptoms, and for some people long-term immunotherapy can reduce sensitivity over time. This article is general information only; anyone with concerns should consult a qualified healthcare professional.
