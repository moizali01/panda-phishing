The Panda's Sixth Finger: An Evolutionary Masterpiece

By Lena Fairweather · June 5, 2026

Pandas appear to have a thumb, but it is not a finger at all. It is a repurposed wrist bone, and it is one of biology's favorite examples of improvised design.

Watch a panda eat and you will see it hold a bamboo stalk with what looks like a thumb pressing against its fingers. But pandas, like other bears, have five regular digits that all point the same way. The 'thumb' is something else entirely.

The structure is an enlarged wrist bone called the radial sesamoid. Over evolutionary time it grew larger and gained muscle attachments, letting the panda press it against the palm to grip bamboo. The famous biologist Stephen Jay Gould wrote a celebrated essay about it, titled 'The Panda's Thumb,' using it as an example of how evolution works with whatever parts are available rather than designing from scratch.

This improvised digit is not elegant in an engineering sense. A true opposable thumb would work better. But evolution could not start over, so it modified an existing bone into a 'good enough' solution that lets pandas handle hundreds of bamboo stalks a day.

Recent fossil discoveries suggest the pseudo-thumb is ancient, appearing in panda ancestors millions of years ago. Interestingly, it never grew very long, possibly because the same bone also helps bear the animal's considerable weight when walking.

The pseudo-thumb is a reminder that nature rarely builds the ideal tool. It builds the possible one. For the panda, a slightly modified wrist bone was all it took to unlock a near-endless food supply.
